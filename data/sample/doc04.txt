The library acquired the Hartley collection in nineteen fifty-four, some eleven years after the death of the collector, and the circumstances of the acquisition have been the subject of periodic argument ever since. Hartley himself left no will that mentioned the collection, and his surviving correspondence suggests that he intended it to go to the university rather than to the municipal library. The university declined, apparently on the grounds of storage cost, and the collection sat in a solicitor's office for the better part of a decade.

What arrived at the library was not what Hartley had catalogued. Comparison of his own inventory with the accession records shows at least forty items unaccounted for, including three of the four manuscripts he considered most significant. Whether these were sold privately during the intervening years, retained by the family, or simply lost in storage is not established. The solicitor's records for the period were destroyed in a fire in nineteen sixty-one, which has proved convenient for everyone who might otherwise have had to explain themselves.

Two of the missing manuscripts surfaced at auction in the nineteen eighties and were bought by an American institution. Their provenance documentation traces them to a dealer in Amsterdam and no further. The library made informal enquiries at the time and was advised that any legal claim would be weak, partly because the library could not demonstrate that the items had ever been in its possession.

The remaining material is substantial and has been well used by researchers. Cataloguing was completed in stages between nineteen sixty-eight and nineteen ninety-four, and the descriptions from the earliest stage are now considered unreliable. A recataloguing project began four years ago and has covered about a third of the collection. It has already identified a dozen items that were misattributed in the original catalogue, two of them significantly.
